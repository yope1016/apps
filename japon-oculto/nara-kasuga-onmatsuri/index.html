On-matsuri de Nara: la procesión de medianoche sin una sola luz y un desfile de 900 años

En una frase: cada diciembre, en Nara, el dios del santuario Wakamiya sale de su templo a las 0:00 del 17 de diciembre, envuelto en humo de antorchas y cantos, en una oscuridad total: no se permiten fotos ni linternas. Al mediodía, un desfile de cortesanos, samuráis y monjes a caballo cruza la ciudad, y por la tarde hay danzas antiguas hasta la noche. El festival se celebra desde 1136 y, según la asociación de turismo de Nara, nunca se ha interrumpido. Verlo desde la calle es gratis.

La historia en 1 minuto

  • 1136: el regente Fujiwara no Tadamichi organizó el primer festival para el santuario Wakamiya, dentro del gran santuario Kasuga Taisha. Las fuentes dan motivos distintos: paz y buenas cosechas (comunicado de 2026), o pedir el fin de hambrunas y epidemias (guía oficial de turismo de Japón).
  • 2026 es la edición 891.
  • Es Bien Cultural Inmaterial Folclórico Importante de Japón (1979).
  • Por eso se le llama «un museo vivo de las artes escénicas»: en un solo día ves kagura, dengaku, bugaku y nō como se ofrecían hace siglos.

El momento secreto: Sengyo no gi (0:00)

A medianoche del 16 al 17, se apagan todas las luces del bosque de Kasuga. Los sacerdotes rodean al dios con ramas de sakaki y lo llevan hasta un santuario provisional (Otabisho) entre antorchas que se arrastran por el suelo y un canto grave y continuo. El sitio oficial lo dice claro: quien asiste no puede tomar fotos ni encender siquiera una linterna. Es la parte que casi ningún turista conoce, y la más impresionante.

Cómo disfrutarlo como local

  1. Elige tu noche: si solo puedes un momento, ve a las 0:00. Llega con tiempo y quédate en silencio; el sonido es la experiencia.
  2. Apaga todo: pantalla del celular, flash, linterna. Si te ven con luz, arruinas el ritual para cientos de personas.
  3. Al mediodía, busca un lugar en Sanjō-dōri o en el camino al primer torii: el desfile es largo y se ve gratis desde la calle.
  4. Por la tarde, el Otabisho: las danzas se ven alrededor del escenario de tierra. Lleva algo caliente: es al aire libre hasta la noche.
  5. Ropa de invierno en serio: en Nara, de madrugada en diciembre, suele hacer temperaturas cercanas a 0 °C.

Nara's On-matsuri: a midnight procession without a single light and a 900-year-old parade

In one line: every December in Nara, the god of Wakamiya shrine leaves its sanctuary at 0:00 on 17 December, surrounded by torch smoke and chanting in total darkness: no photos and no flashlights allowed. At noon, a parade of courtiers, samurai and monks on horseback crosses the city, and in the afternoon ancient dances run into the night. The festival has been held since 1136 and, according to the Nara tourism association, has never been interrupted. Watching from the street is free.

The story in 1 minute

  • 1136: Regent Fujiwara no Tadamichi held the first festival for Wakamiya shrine, inside the great Kasuga Taisha. Sources give different reasons: peace and good harvests (2026 press release), or prayers against famine and epidemics (Japan's official tourism guide).
  • 2026 is the 891st edition.
  • It is an Important Intangible Folk Cultural Property of Japan (1979).
  • That's why it's called "a living museum of performing arts": in one day you see kagura, dengaku, bugaku and Noh as they were offered centuries ago.

The secret moment: Sengyo no gi (0:00)

At midnight between the 16th and 17th, every light in the Kasuga forest goes out. Priests surround the god with sakaki branches and carry it to a temporary shrine (Otabisho) among torches dragged along the ground and a deep, unbroken chant. The official site is clear: attendees may not take photos or even switch on a flashlight. It is the part almost no tourist knows about, and the most powerful.

Paid seats (2026)

  • Noborioji (parade route): ¥2,000 · 220 seats · 11:30–12:30
  • Matsu-no-shita-shiki: south side ¥5,500 / north side ¥5,000
  • In front of the Otabisho: ¥10,000 · 160 seats · 13:00–22:30 · includes guide booklet, blanket and audio guide
  • Book online via the Nara tourism association's "NARA Time" until 14 December (or by phone, 0742-30-0230, weekdays 9–17). Unsold seats go on sale on the day.
  • In bad weather the parade and those seats are cancelled; the Otabisho seats continue in light rain.

How to enjoy it like a local

  1. Pick your night: if you can see only one moment, go at 0:00. Arrive early and stay silent; the sound is the experience.
  2. Switch everything off: phone screen, flash, flashlight. A single light spoils the rite for hundreds of people.
  3. At noon, find a spot on Sanjō-dōri or along the way to the first torii: the parade is long and free to watch from the street.
  4. In the afternoon, the Otabisho: the dances are performed around an earthen stage. Bring something warm: it is outdoors until night.
  5. Serious winter clothing: in Nara, early mornings in December are often close to 0 °C.

⚠️ Before you go

  • During Sengyo no gi and Kankō no gi no photos and no lights are allowed (official site).
  • Exact times can change; check the official programme (onmatsuri.kasugataisha.or.jp).
  • There is no published official fee for watching from the street; guides list it as free. Seats are optional and paid.
  • Late at night, transport back to the station is sparse: plan to walk back to Kintetsu Nara Station.
  • Information as of 14 November 2026.

🎐 Getting there

  • From JR Nara or Kintetsu Nara stations: bus towards "Kasuga Taisha Honden" (about 11–15 min) or on foot (about 20–30 min through Nara Park).
  • The parade starts in front of Nara Prefectural Office, a few minutes' walk from Kintetsu Nara.

🎐 Combine it with

  • Naramachi: wooden houses on an Asuka-era temple, a short walk from the Otabisho.
  • Wakakusa Yamayaki: in January, the night the mountain behind Kasuga burns.
  • Asuka: burial mounds and the cradle of Japan, under an hour by train.
