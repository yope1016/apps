Jōgi: el templo del «único deseo de tu vida» y su tofu frito triangular

En una frase: a una hora de bus del centro de Sendai, en un valle de montaña, está el templo Jōgi Nyorai Saihōji (定義如来 西方寺). Según la tradición, aquí se escondió un samurái del clan Taira que huyó tras perder la guerra en 1185, y el lugar tomó el nombre que él usó para ocultarse. Hoy la gente viene a pedir «el único gran deseo de su vida», la entrada es gratis, y en la calle del templo venden un tofu frito triangular recién hecho por 180 ¥ que los de Sendai conocen de memoria.

Por qué vale la pena

Jōgi tiene todo lo que hace especial a un «pueblo de templo» japonés, pero sin turistas extranjeros: una calle corta con tiendas de dulces, el olor a aceite caliente del tofu, abuelas que llegan en bus a pedir por sus nietos y un bosque de montaña alrededor. La historia del samurái fugitivo que se esconde con un nombre falso le da al lugar un aire de novela, y el gran salón hexagonal de madera es impresionante por dentro.

El tofu frito es la estrella. En Sendai mucha gente asocia la palabra «Jōgi» directamente con el abura-age triangular: hay días en que la tienda vende miles. Lo pides en el mostrador, te lo dan en un plato con palillos, le pones shichimi y soja de las botellas que están ahí, y te lo comes de pie, quemándote un poco los dedos.

El guiño latino: es como ir a la Basílica de Guadalupe y comerte unas gorditas de La Villa a la salida: la peregrinación y el antojo van juntos. Aquí la gente viene a pedir un milagro grande, y la recompensa del camino es un tofu frito caliente en una mañana fría de montaña.

Cómo hacerlo como los que saben

  1. Toma el bus temprano: hay un bus cada 1–2 horas y el último de regreso sale por la tarde. Mira el horario del bus municipal de Sendai la víspera y calcula medio día en total.
  2. Primero el tofu, luego el templo: la tienda está junto a la entrada. Pide uno «recién frito» y come ahí mismo; si llevas a casa, también venden paquetes.
  3. Entra al salón principal y siéntate un momento: no hace falta pedir una oración formal. Si quieres hacerlo, llega antes de las 15:30.
  4. Busca el Sadayoshi-dō detrás, el pequeño salón hexagonal sobre la tumba del samurái. Ahí se puede copiar sutras (shakyō).
  5. No toques los árboles del Tennō-zuka: son sagrados. Mira cómo los dos troncos se unieron.
  6. Si vas el día 7 (marzo–noviembre), la pagoda se abre y puedes ver su imagen de frente.
  7. Combínalo con Sakunami o Akiu, zonas de onsen en el camino, o vuelve a Sendai para cenar lengua de res gyūtan.

⚠️ Antes de ir

  • De diciembre a marzo hay nieve y hielo en la zona: zapatos con buen agarre; si vas en auto, neumáticos de invierno.
  • Es un lugar de oración: habla bajo dentro de los salones y pregunta antes de fotografiar el interior. Los drones están prohibidos sin permiso.

Jōgi: the temple of your "one wish in a lifetime" and its triangle fried tofu

In one line: an hour by bus from central Sendai, in a mountain valley, stands Jōgi Nyorai Saihōji (定義如来 西方寺). Tradition says a samurai of the Taira clan hid here after losing the war in 1185, and the place took the alias he used. Today people come to ask for "the one great wish of their life", entry is free, and on the temple street a shop sells freshly fried triangle tofu for ¥180 that every Sendai local knows.

🙏 Key facts

WhatDetail
What it isA Jōdo (Pure Land) Buddhist temple in Ōkura, Aoba-ku, Sendai. Its main image is a painted scroll of Amida (the "Jōgi Nyorai"), kept as a hidden image. It's famous because, by popular belief, it grants one great wish in your lifetime
The legendAfter the Taira clan's defeat at Dan-no-ura (1185), the warrior Taira no Sadayoshi (平貞能), a retainer of Taira no Shigemori, fled north with an Amida scroll and hid in this valley. To avoid capture he changed his name to "Jōgi" (定義) — an alternative reading of his own name (貞能). The place kept the name. Temple tradition says he died here in 1198 and his followers built a shrine; Saihōji was formally founded in 1706
What to seeThe Sanmon gate (1932, by carpenter Hanawa Kikuzō, with guardian kings and the three wise monkeys carved in); the Sadayoshi-dō, a hexagonal hall from 1927 over the samurai's grave (the main hall until 1999, now used for sutra copying); the five-storied pagoda (1986, about 29–30 m); the great hexagonal main hall (1999, 18 m tall, Aomori hiba cypress); and the Tennō-zuka, two zelkova trees that grew into one, revered for love and children
Entry and hoursFree, open daily. Main-hall grounds approx. 7:45–16:15, pagoda area from 8:00 (some sources say 7:30). Prayers (gokitō) are requested at the main hall until about 15:30
The 7thFrom March to November, on the 7th of each month, the pagoda is opened so you can see its image face to face

Why go

Jōgi has everything that makes a Japanese "temple town" special, without foreign crowds: a short street of sweet shops, the smell of hot oil from the tofu, grandmothers arriving by bus to pray for their grandchildren, and mountain forest all around. The tale of a fugitive samurai hiding under a false name gives it the air of a novel, and the great hexagonal wooden hall is striking inside.

The fried tofu is the star. In Sendai many people hear "Jōgi" and think of the triangle abura-age: on busy days the shop sells thousands. You order at the counter, get it on a plate with chopsticks, add shichimi and soy from the bottles there, and eat it standing up, burning your fingers a little.

The Latin American link: it's like visiting the Basilica of Guadalupe and grabbing gorditas de La Villa on the way out: the pilgrimage and the snack go together. Here people come to ask for one big miracle, and the reward for the trip is hot fried tofu on a cold mountain morning.

Insider tips

  1. Take an early bus: buses run every 1–2 hours and the last one back leaves in the afternoon. Check the Sendai City Bus timetable the day before and plan half a day in total.
  2. Tofu first, then the temple: the shop is next to the entrance. Ask for one "freshly fried" and eat it right there; packs to take home are sold too.
  3. Step into the main hall and sit for a moment: you don't need a formal prayer. If you want one, arrive before 15:30.
  4. Find the Sadayoshi-dō behind it, the small hexagonal hall over the samurai's grave, where you can copy sutras (shakyō).
  5. Don't touch the Tennō-zuka trees: they are sacred. Look at how the two trunks joined.
  6. On the 7th (March–November), the pagoda opens and you can see its image face to face.
  7. Combine it with Sakunami or Akiu, onsen areas on the way, or head back to Sendai for gyūtan beef tongue.

⚠️ Before you go

  • From December to March there is snow and ice in the area: wear shoes with good grip; if driving, use winter tyres.
  • This is a place of prayer: speak softly inside the halls and ask before photographing interiors. Drones are banned without permission.
  • Official information is almost all in Japanese. Look for "定義" to spot the right bus.
