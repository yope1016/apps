Wasanbon: el azúcar que se amasa a mano en invierno y se deshace en la lengua

En una frase: en el este de Kagawa, a unos 40 minutos de Takamatsu, todavía se hace el wasanbon (和三盆), un azúcar tan fino que parece harina y que se derrite en la boca como nieve. Sale de una caña delgada y antigua, el chikutō (竹糖, «caña bambú»), y se pule a mano, con agua, sobre una bandeja, prensándolo con piedras entre una pasada y otra. Una casa de 1804 lo sigue haciendo así, y en el pueblo de Hiketa puedes moldear tus propios dulces y comértelos con un té matcha.

Por qué vale la pena

Si alguna vez comiste un wagashi en Kioto y sentiste que el azúcar «desaparecía» en vez de empalagar, probablemente era wasanbon. Casi nadie sabe que viene de aquí, de una franja de costa tranquila entre Takamatsu y Naruto, donde el viajero solo pasa de largo en tren.

Lo especial no es solo el sabor, sino el gesto: el azúcar se trabaja con las manos, mojado, sobre una bandeja de madera, como quien amasa pan, y se exprime con piedras colgadas de una palanca. Cada pasada lo hace más blanco y más fino, pero sin quitarle del todo el aroma de la caña. En el taller de Hiketa, ese polvo se aprieta en moldes de madera tallados a mano —flores, peces, figuras de temporada— hechos por Yoshihiro Ichihara, artesano tradicional y el único que hace estos moldes en Shikoku. Sacas el dulce del molde, lo pones en el plato y te lo comes al momento con un matcha; el resto te lo llevas en una cajita.

El guiño latino: en América Latina el azúcar de caña «sin refinar» tiene mil nombres: panela en Colombia, piloncillo en México, chancaca en Perú y Chile, rapadura en Brasil. Todos guardan la melaza. El wasanbon es el camino contrario: le van quitando la melaza a mano, poco a poco, hasta dejar solo un recuerdo de ella. Es como si alguien hubiera tomado un pilón de piloncillo y lo hubiera pulido durante una semana hasta convertirlo en nieve.

Cómo hacerlo como los que saben

  1. Ve entre diciembre y febrero si quieres coincidir con la temporada de producción (la caña se cosecha en diciembre). El taller de moldes funciona todo el año.
  2. Escribe o llama antes a Izutsu Yashiki para confirmar el horario del taller, el precio y si hace falta reservar; los miércoles suele cerrar.
  3. Prueba primero el azúcar solo: ponte una pizca en la lengua y espera. Luego compara con un dulce moldeado.
  4. Pasea por Hiketa: el barrio alrededor de Izutsu Yashiki conserva casas antiguas de comerciantes; la visita a pie es corta y tranquila.
  5. Compra para regalar: los dulces de wasanbon son ligeros, duran y viajan bien en la maleta.
  6. Combina con Naruto (Tokushima), al otro lado de la frontera: el museo Ōtsuka queda a menos de una hora por carretera.

Wasanbon: the sugar kneaded by hand in winter that melts on your tongue

In one line: in eastern Kagawa, about 40 minutes from Takamatsu, people still make wasanbon (和三盆), a sugar so fine it looks like flour and melts in your mouth like snow. It comes from a thin, old cane called chikutō (竹糖, "bamboo cane") and is polished by hand, with water, on a tray, pressed under weights between rounds. A house founded in 1804 still makes it this way, and in the town of Hiketa you can press your own sweets in wooden molds and eat them with matcha.

What it isUltra-fine cane sugar from Kagawa and Tokushima. Kagawa's is whiter and lighter; Tokushima's (Awa wasanbon) is slightly yellow with more molasses flavor
The caneChikutō (Saccharum sinense): thinner stalks than common cane, with less juice. Today it is grown only in parts of Kagawa and Tokushima. It reached Sanuki (old Kagawa) in the late 18th century, by the leading theory
The name"Wa" = Japanese, "san" = three, "bon" = tray: it is said the sugar was once kneaded three times on the tray
SeasonThe cane is planted in March and harvested and pressed from late November to December; the sugar work follows. In other words: now is when it is made
How it's madeThe juice is boiled and skimmed day and night, then crystallizes in clay jars (about 2 weeks) into shirashita-tō. It is then "polished" by hand with water (研ぎ, togi) and pressed in an oshibune (押し船, a wooden press with weights) to draw off molasses. This repeats about 5 times over several days

Why it's worth it

If you've ever eaten a wagashi in Kyoto and felt the sugar "vanish" instead of cloying, it was probably wasanbon. Few people know it comes from here, a quiet strip of coast between Takamatsu and Naruto that travelers only see from the train window.

The magic is not only the taste but the gesture: the sugar is worked by hand, damp, on a wooden tray, like kneading bread, then squeezed under stones hung from a lever. Each round makes it whiter and finer without fully losing the aroma of the cane. At the Hiketa workshop, the powder is pressed into hand-carved wooden molds — flowers, fish, seasonal shapes — made by Yoshihiro Ichihara, a certified traditional craftsman and the only maker of these molds in Shikoku. You tap the sweet out, set it on a plate and eat it right away with matcha; the rest goes home in a little box.

The Latin American angle: in Latin America, unrefined cane sugar has a thousand names: panela in Colombia, piloncillo in Mexico, chancaca in Peru and Chile, rapadura in Brazil. All of them keep the molasses. Wasanbon goes the other way: the molasses is drawn off by hand, little by little, until only a memory of it remains. Imagine someone polishing a cone of piloncillo for a week until it turns into snow.

Do it like an insider

  1. Go between December and February to catch the production season (the cane is harvested in December). The molding workshop runs all year.
  2. Call or write ahead to Izutsu Yashiki to confirm workshop hours, price and whether booking is needed; it usually closes on Wednesdays.
  3. Taste the plain sugar first: put a pinch on your tongue and wait. Then compare it with a molded sweet.
  4. Walk around Hiketa: the streets around Izutsu Yashiki keep old merchant houses; it's a short, quiet stroll.
  5. Buy gifts: wasanbon sweets are light, keep well and travel easily in a suitcase.
  6. Pair it with Naruto (Tokushima), just over the border: the Ōtsuka Museum is under an hour by road.

  • Workshop price and booking: not published in the sources we checked. Confirm with Sanshū Izutsu Yashiki (0879-23-8550) before going.
  • Shop hours inside Izutsu Yashiki vary; some close on Wednesdays.
  • Mitani Seitō is a working producer with a shop: the shop opens 9:00–18:00, but seeing the sugar work is not guaranteed. Ask first.
  • Express trains to Hiketa are not very frequent: check the JR Shikoku timetable for your return.
  • Diet: it is almost pure sugar; keep that in mind if you watch your blood sugar.
